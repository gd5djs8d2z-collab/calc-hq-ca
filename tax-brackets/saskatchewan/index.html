Saskatchewan Tax Brackets 2026

Saskatchewan gets by with three income tax rates, 10.5%, 12.5% and 14.5%, and it starts higher than most provinces do. What offsets that is a $20,381 basic personal amount, which the Saskatchewan Affordability Act is raising by $500 a year on top of indexation. These are the 2026 federal and Saskatchewan brackets, and the combined rate each band of income pays.

Three provincial rates, five federal ones

Each schedule taxes the same taxable income, a slice at a time. Saskatchewan's middle rate begins at $54,532, just short of the federal $58,523, and its top rate at $155,805, well before Ottawa's top two brackets.

Both thresholds, and the personal amount, rose this January by Saskatchewan's indexing factor. With only two thresholds, the province's schedule has fewer places where a raise can tip income into a new rate than anywhere else except Manitoba, which also has three brackets.

A higher first rate, a larger personal amount

At 10.5%, Saskatchewan's first bracket is about twice Ontario's and nearly double British Columbia's. Its personal amount does a lot of the evening out. Valued at that same 10.5%, the $20,381 credit cancels $2,140 of Saskatchewan tax, so a resident claiming nothing but the basic amount owes the province nothing on the first $20,381. That is $3,929 more income free of provincial tax than the federal personal amount leaves free of federal tax.

The Affordability Act increases apply to the basic personal amount, the spouse and equivalent-to-spouse amounts, the dependent child amount and the senior supplementary amount, and they come in addition to the normal indexation. Each one moves this table's lowest rows: the band taxed at federal rates alone gets wider every year it applies.

The combined rate, band by band

Nothing in Saskatchewan's own schedule bends the rates the way a surtax or a tax reduction would elsewhere, so the rows below are mostly the federal and Saskatchewan rates added together. The figures are federal plus Saskatchewan income tax on income from interest, rent or a pension, where no CPP or EI applies.

A salary sees lower figures in the middle rows. Up to $85,000, a dollar of pay carries its own CPP and EI credit, and the enhanced CPP on it is deductible, so at $70,000 the next dollar of pay costs 31.46% where the table says 33.00%. Once pay passes $85,000 the two match again, and each threshold in the table sits $1,127 lower than the salary that reaches it.

$70,000 in Regina or Saskatoon

A $70,000 salary draws $7,278 of federal tax and $4,973 of Saskatchewan tax, $12,251 between them, which is 17.5% of the pay. CPP and EI take $5,080 more. Saskatchewan's share is close to two thirds of the federal one, even though its rate on this salary's top dollar, 12.5%, is well under Ottawa's 20.5%.

Common questions

What are the Saskatchewan tax brackets for 2026?

10.5% on taxable income up to $54,532, 12.5% up to $155,805, and 14.5% above that. Add federal tax and the top-bracket rate comes to 47.5%.

What is Saskatchewan's basic personal amount for 2026?

$20,381. It is indexed each January, and the Saskatchewan Affordability Act adds $500 a year on top of indexation for four tax years. At 10.5% it cancels $2,140 of provincial tax.

Is my marginal rate the same as my bracket?

Not quite. Your bracket is the Saskatchewan rate on your top dollar; your marginal rate adds the federal rate and anything that changes with income. At $54,532 to $58,523 of taxable income the combined rate is 26.50%, of which 12.5% is Saskatchewan's.
