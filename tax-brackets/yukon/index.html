Yukon Tax Brackets 2026

Yukon builds its income tax on Ottawa's frame. Its first three thresholds are the federal ones to the dollar, its basic personal amount is the federal amount and shrinks the same way, and only above $500,000 does it add a bracket of its own. These are Yukon's 2026 brackets beside the federal schedule, and the combined rate on each band, which for that reason is unusually easy to read.

Five territorial rates on the federal thresholds

Both governments tax the same taxable income, each rate applying only inside its band. Line the two tables up and the first three thresholds match: $58,523, $117,045 and $181,440 start a new rate in both schedules at once.

Because Yukon indexes those thresholds by the same factor as Ottawa each January, the two schedules move together. The exception is the top bracket. Yukon's 15% rate starts at a flat $500,000, a figure that isn't on the federal schedule, while Ottawa's top rate of 33% begins at $258,482.

A personal amount borrowed from Ottawa

Yukon's basic personal amount is the federal one: $16,452 for net income up to $181,440, falling to $14,829 by $258,482. Valued at Yukon's lowest rate of 6.4%, the full amount cancels $1,053 of territorial tax.

Two things follow. Federal and Yukon tax both start on the same dollar, so there is no band where one government taxes and the other doesn't, as there is in most provinces. And between $181,440 and $258,482 both amounts shrink together, which adds 0.43% to the combined rate rather than the federal taper alone.

Yukon also gives a territorial credit for the Canada employment amount, which none of the provinces do. On employment income it is worth $96 of Yukon tax at most.

The whole range in seven rows

Each row marks a point where calc-hq's engine, stepped upward through incomes, found the price of an extra dollar changing. With the thresholds and personal amounts shared, almost every change happens at the same place in both schedules, which is why the table is this short. It shows federal and Yukon income tax together on income that has no CPP or EI attached, for example interest, rent or a pension.

On wages the rate is lower until $85,000, since wages come with CPP and EI contributions that earn credits of their own, and the enhanced CPP is subtracted from taxable income. Past that point the salary rate equals the table's, except that each boundary arrives $1,127 later in gross pay. The Canada employment amount doesn't change the rate; it lowers tax by a fixed amount once earnings are high enough to claim it in full.

Yukon is also a prescribed northern zone, so most residents can take the northern residents deduction on their federal return. It shrinks taxable income, which can put you in an earlier, cheaper row of the table, but no rate in it changes. The engine's figures don't include it.

$120,000 in Whitehorse

A $120,000 salary is taxed $17,502 federally and $7,766 by Yukon, $25,268 in all and 21.1% of the pay. Its next dollar is taxed at 36.90%: 26% federal plus 10.9% territorial, since the income has crossed $117,045 on both schedules.

Common questions

Does Yukon use the federal tax brackets?

For its first three thresholds, yes: $58,523, $117,045 and $181,440 start a new rate in both. Yukon's rates are its own, from 6.4% to 15%, and its top bracket starts at $500,000, a threshold the federal schedule doesn't have.

What is Yukon's top combined tax rate?

48% on income over $500,000: the federal 33% plus Yukon's 15%. Between $258,482 and $500,000 it is 45.80%.

Is Yukon's basic personal amount the same as the federal one?

Yes. It is $16,452 for net income up to $181,440 and declines to $14,829 at $258,482, exactly as the federal amount does, but it is valued at Yukon's 6.4% instead of the federal 14%.
